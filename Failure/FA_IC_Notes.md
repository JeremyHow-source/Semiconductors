# Failure Analysis of Integrated Circuits — Reading Notes

## Electrical Fault Isolation

- **[Ch.7]** Two backside approaches can provide the timing information essential for speed path measurements in design debug or failure analysis of speed-sensitive problems: backside drilling followed by mechanical or e-beam probing, and IR (infrared) modulation-based optical beam probing. *(2026-06-23)*
- **[Ch.7]** Backside FIB milling can drill precisely positioned holes with an inverted pyramid profile (effective aspect ratio of ~1:1) to expose metal-1 or polysilicon conductors for probing. This low aspect ratio facilitates probing without extraordinary probe-point creation techniques and is useful for flip-chip prototype repair. Precise positioning is achieved by overlaying a backside IR optical image onto the FIB image, using the FIB milled hole (visible in both images) for final alignment. Subsequent e-beam or mechanical probing is slow and tedious, but it remains the only way to get quantitative DC or AC voltage amplitude measurements from flip-chip packaged ICs (since IR modulation-based probing provides only relative signals). *(2026-06-23)*
- **[Ch.7]** During IR modulation-based probing, as the IR beam passes through the diffusion region of a transistor, three types of modulation can occur depending on geometry, materials involved, and the current/voltage state of the device: (1) amplitude modulation, (2) phase shift or propagation delay, and (3) polarization change. *(2026-06-23)*
- **[Ch.7]** The measurement bandwidth during IR modulation probing is limited by the laser pulse width and stimulus source jitter stability. Typical laser pulse widths and jitter yield a ~50 ps measurement bandwidth, which is capable of making precise timing and propagation delay measurements required for design debug. *(2026-06-23)*
- **[Ch.7]** Polarization-based IR modulation only works on optically active materials such as GaAs, giving it relatively limited applicability (e.g., for super high-speed GaAs). Phase shift-based modulation detects changes in the active region's refractive index resulting from charge carrier density changes, most prevalent in bipolar or current-mode logic (CML) devices. These refractive index changes cause subtle beam propagation delays, better described as optical phase shifts, measurable interferometrically by passing a reference beam through a neighbouring non-active area of silicon — which also cancels thermal modulation errors. Limitation: modulation is very weak in conventional CMOS devices, since carrier density changes by a relatively small amount as the CMOS circuit switches. *(2026-06-23)*
- **[Ch.7]** Amplitude-based IR modulation exploits changes in the electric field across a PN junction. A reverse-biased junction produces a subtle shift in the effective band gap energy, which causes a slight change in absorption — known as the **Franz-Keldysh effect**. When the IR probe wavelength (~1.06 µm) is close to the material's band gap energy, it can be used for waveform measurement. This technique is field-strength based with a modulation depth on the order of 1 part in ~100,000, making it applicable to conventional CMOS circuits. However, acquisition speed with this relatively weak signal remains a challenge; noise suppression and signal processing are key implementation factors. *(2026-06-23)*

- **[Ch.7]** Application tips for IR modulation-based probing: IR transmission through the silicon substrate decreases exponentially with thickness, so thinning is very beneficial provided mechanical integrity and thermal dissipation are not sacrificed. Anti-reflection coatings on a polished substrate can increase the reflected IR power by up to 50%, due to the optical impedance mismatch between the refractive index of air (n = 1) and the relatively high bulk silicon IR refractive index (n ≈ 3.5). *(2026-06-23)*
- **[Ch.7]** The majority of heat generated by the IC escapes through the substrate due to the relatively good thermal transmission characteristics of bulk silicon. Thermal mass for dissipating power is significantly reduced by thinning the silicon and removing the heat sink (if one is attached to the back of the device). *(2026-06-23)*

## Physical / Structural Analysis

- **[Ch.10]** In a standard optical microscope, the purpose of the objective lens is to create a focal plane image of the device being inspected, achieved by placing a camera or detector in the focal plane. The image can also be recreated with an ocular lens to be focused on the retina for direct viewing. *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.10]** Depth of field is predominantly influenced by the same factors as resolution. As resolution improves (decreases), depth of field becomes smaller. Confocal microscopy provides two techniques to overcome this limitation by integrating images of differing focal planes. *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.10]** Working distance is a significant parameter for optical microscopy applications. Many failure analysis applications require long working distance objective lenses for inspection of devices in a package. High numerical aperture (NA) lenses tend to have very short working distances; an objective lens farther away from the sample will collect less light, giving it a lower NA. This is not a serious issue for imaging wafers. *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.10]** However, short working distances can limit inspection of packaged devices. Packaged device failure analysis takes advantage of longer working distance lenses, which may have slightly lower numerical aperture. Lenses for mechanical probing tend to be ultra-long working distance to accommodate probes, yielding the poorest resolution but the best depth of field. *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.10]** Disadvantages of IR microscopy include several inherent factors: the wavelength is longer than visible light and is diffraction-limited, so the resolution of IR microscopy is not as good as visible light microscopy. Despite this limitation, the combination of migration to flip-chip packaging and increases in the number of interconnect layers make backside viewing through silicon a highly desirable feature. `#IR` `#flip-chip` `#backside-inspection` `#resolution` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.10]** For wavelengths of light slightly above the bandgap of silicon, IR light is used as an active probe — the light has enough energy to generate electron–hole pair formation. At longer wavelengths (less energy), IR light does not have enough energy to create carriers and can provide an electrically passive probe. `#IR` `#bandgap` `#active-probe` `#passive-probe` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.10]** Atomic number contributes to secondary electron contrast, but the impact is small compared to its effect on backscattered electrons (BSE), which provide an alternative imaging method. Backscattered electrons are incident electrons that have bounced off nuclei in the sample; the primary contrast mechanism is atomic number, since electrons are much more likely to be backscattered from a large nucleus than a small one. BSEs typically have higher energy than secondary electrons, with the centre of energy distribution at 50–95% of the incident electron energy. `#SEM` `#BSE` `#atomic-number-contrast` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.10]** Backscattered electrons can be readily detected with a standard SEM electron detector, with secondary electrons rejected by a negatively biased grid. As long as there is a line of sight between the detector and the sample, backscattered electrons will contribute to SEM images, providing much of the atomic number contrast. `#SEM` `#BSE` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.10]** Backscattered electron imaging commonly uses segmented solid-state detectors mounted at the top of the SEM chamber, with samples inspected flat. Due to the directional nature of backscattered electrons — they are not significantly deflected by local electrical fields — BSE images depend on the direction of the detector. This proves useful for analyses involving heavy metals, such as detection of lead-rich areas of solder. `#SEM` `#BSE` `#solid-state-detector` `#solder` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.10]** SEM variables: the key variable is the incident electron energy or accelerating potential of the beam. Since electrons exhibit particle–wave behaviour, they have a wavelength, which results in diffraction-limited resolution. The wavelength of electrons is inversely proportional to the energy of the electrons in the SEM. `#SEM` `#accelerating-voltage` `#resolution` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.10]** The advantages of higher accelerating potential for resolution are often offset by other factors: more energy is available for creating secondary radiation, resulting in secondary electrons exiting the sample. Higher energy beams can lead to more secondary electron emission from outside the incident electron spot, which degrades spatial resolution and causes greater sample charging. `#SEM` `#accelerating-voltage` `#charging` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.10]** Available beam energies are typically in the 1–25 keV range. Beam energies of 5–25 keV are most commonly used when sample charging is not an issue; lower energies of 1–5 keV are used for samples that charge. `#SEM` `#accelerating-voltage` `#charging` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.10]** The next variable affecting electron beam applications is beam current. Reducing beam current allows creation of a smaller spot with higher resolution; at the same time, reduced beam current degrades the signal-to-noise ratio, which has a major impact on contrast and image quality. `#SEM` `#beam-current` `#resolution` `#SNR` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.10]** Within electron beam instruments that observe lower cross-section events (such as Auger), higher beam currents are required for an acceptable signal. Poor signal-to-noise results in a grainy image that needs to be improved by averaging several images. Navigation on a sample requires a real-time image, particularly in repetitive structures. As such, beam current is usually set to a minimum value that generates an acceptable signal-to-noise ratio. `#SEM` `#Auger` `#beam-current` `#SNR` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.10]** Working distance in the SEM has a significant impact on resolution; ideally imaging should be performed at a very short working distance. The electron optics or lenses serve to focus the electron beam to a spot. `#SEM` `#working-distance` `#resolution` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.10]** When working distance increases, the distance from the optics also increases and the beam becomes more difficult to control and focus. The highest resolution SEMs place the sample inside the final electron lens; such "in-the-lens" SEMs are limited by a small sample size requirement. A problem created by using very short working distance is the collection of secondary electrons — it tends to cut off the path from the sample to the secondary electron detector. The in-the-lens configuration provides a way to maximise signal collection. `#SEM` `#working-distance` `#in-the-lens` `#resolution` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.10]** Increased tilt angle also serves as a useful augmentation: secondary electron intensity increases with tilt angle, providing better signal-to-noise and the three-dimensional perspective of SEM. However, larger samples can result in very long working distances when increasing the sample tilt. `#SEM` `#tilt` `#SNR` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.10]** SEM sample preparation is generally centred on reducing charging effects; grounding of the sample is a key factor in obtaining high-quality images and provides a mechanism for bleeding off accumulated charge. The sample holder is typically grounded through the SEM hardware. Attachment and grounding to the holder can be achieved in several ways — mechanical attachment such as clips should be preferred over conductive paints (typically silver or carbon), since such paints can degrade the sample chamber vacuum. `#SEM` `#sample-prep` `#charging` `#grounding` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.10]** Establishing a discharge path from the device surface through the sample is made difficult by the dielectrics in the IC, which can prevent discharge — particularly for deprocessed devices where connections through bond wires and metal interconnects are severed. The preferred alternative is to use low beam energies, usually around 1 keV. `#SEM` `#charging` `#deprocessing` `#low-kV` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.10]** When low beam energy is not possible or not effective in reducing charging, conductive coating can be used. Very thin films (a few monolayers) of a conductive material are typically sputtered onto the sample. Chromium is the preferred coating material for FESEM. Previously, gold or its alloy constituents were used prior to FESEM — gold deposits in islands that are not resolved by SEMs with thermionic emission sources but are resolved by FESEM. Carbon coatings are used when EDS analysis is to be performed. `#SEM` `#FESEM` `#charging` `#coating` `#EDS` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.10]** Focused ion beam (FIB) imaging: FIB has become standard for probe point creation and design rewiring, and is preferred for both SEM and TEM cross-section preparation. It provides very high sensitivity chemical analysis in SIMS mode; however, its spot sizes are significantly larger than for a SEM, resulting in poorer spatial resolution. `#FIB` `#SIMS` `#resolution` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.10]** Future considerations: optical microscopy is the only technique that provides effective inspection of buried defects in highly planarised devices. UV microscopes can be expected to use all available means to achieve optimum resolution — confocal, laser, immersion lenses, etc. Furthermore, there is a concern that a higher percentage of defects will not be observable with microscopy (classified as No Visual Defect, NVD). For example, as the number of doping atoms used to form a junction becomes relatively small, statistical variations in the number of dopant atoms could impact the electrical performance of the transistor. This category of defect is impossible to detect with standard microscopes and would require a scanning capacitance microscope (SCM) to provide insight into localised electrical characteristics with very high spatial resolution. `#optical` `#UV` `#NVD` `#SCM` `#future` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.10]** Most analytical techniques can be viewed in terms of four critical elements: (1) incident radiation; (2) the physical interaction of the incident radiation with the sample; (3) radiation flux from the sample resulting from that interaction — either a new form of radiation excited by the incident radiation or an attenuation of the incident radiation; and (4) a detector for the radiation flux from the sample. `#analytical-framework` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.10]** Incident radiation is usually the dominant factor in determining the spatial resolution of a technique. Within chemical analysis, spatial resolution can be loosely defined as the smallest area of the sample surface that can be analysed with minimal interference from surrounding material. `#analytical-framework` `#spatial-resolution` *(2026-06-25)*

- **[Ch.11]** Energy Dispersive X-ray analysis (EDS) is the primary chemical analysis tool for failure analysis as a general-purpose technique. Its advantage is that it is used in conjunction with a SEM which is already available. It has excellent spatial resolution with good sensitivity and can perform analysis quickly and easily. Interpretation of spectra is relatively easy and can be automated. Quality of spatial resolution arises from the small spot size of the SEM, and the ability to move the electron beam enables dot mapping and line scanning. `#EDS` `#SEM` `#chemical-analysis` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** The depth of penetration for the incident electron beam is typically several microns only; since the escape cross-section for X-rays is high, analysis is performed of the material up to that depth in the sample. The surface is therefore a relatively small part of the sampled volume, making X-ray analysis of limited value for very thin surface films. Sensitivity of the technique is on the order of 0.1% for most elements, but less for light elements. `#EDS` `#penetration-depth` `#sensitivity` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** Primary disadvantages of EDS are moderate sensitivity (especially for light elements), limited applicability to surface analysis, and that it provides only atomic information without the ability to provide molecular information. `#EDS` `#limitations` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** Wavelength Dispersive Spectroscopy (WDS) detects the same interaction as EDS — the type of data collected is identical. However, the different method of detection improves the resolution of X-ray peaks and yields improved signal-to-noise ratios, resulting in better sensitivity. The drawback is that analysis time is long relative to EDS. `#WDS` `#EDS` `#sensitivity` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** EDS is limited for the study of surface interactions because of its relatively high depth of penetration; Auger electron spectroscopy (AES) often fills this gap. EDS sensitivity is moderate — applications requiring high sensitivity commonly employ SIMS instead. Atomic information provided by EDS is usually adequate for analysis of inorganic materials; however, when molecular information is required to identify organic contamination, microspot FTIR is employed. `#EDS` `#Auger` `#SIMS` `#FTIR` `#surface-analysis` *(2026-06-25)*

  | Technique | Incident Radiation | Detected Radiation | Spatial Resolution | Sensitivity | Primary Use |

  | WDS       | Electrons         | X-rays            | ~1 µm             | ~0.01%      | Improved sensitivity over EDS |

- **[Ch.11]** Organic contaminants are widespread and cover a vast range of varied materials, difficult to differentiate based on atomic composition alone since they are made up predominantly of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, and nitrogen. These are typically determined by both SIMS and FTIR, except for hydrogen which is not detectable by energy dispersive analysis or Auger. `#FTIR` `#organic-contamination` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** The presence of other elements such as halogens or sulfur can provide important clues as to the precise organic compound present, but it is often inconclusive since these elements are typically present only at a low atomic percent, if at all. `#FTIR` `#organic-contamination` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** Infrared spectroscopy provides a method of identifying organic building blocks present in a contaminant molecule through bending, stretching, and vibration transitions within chemical bonds. Organic molecules have well-defined energy levels associated with these transitions; when infrared light having exactly the energy required to cause a transition between these states is absorbed, each type of bond produces an absorption spectrum typical of that bond. The exact energy value is affected by what is attached to the atoms forming the bond. `#FTIR` `#IR-spectroscopy` `#molecular-identification` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** Example: a carbon–oxygen double bond (C=O) exhibits absorption in a narrow range, with the exact energy depending on the group attached to the carbon. Aldehydes (one hydrogen atom and one organic group attached to the carbon) have slightly different absorptions from a ketone (two organic groups attached to the carbon). Similarly, absorptions for an acid (an organic group and –OH group attached to the carbon) will be different. `#FTIR` `#C=O` `#functional-groups` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** Initial interpretation of an IR spectrum involves identifying the types of bonds present, which might include: carbon–carbon bonds (single, double, and triple), carbon–nitrogen bonds (single, double, and triple), aromatic groups (benzene derivatives), and carbon–oxygen bonds (single and double). Initial identification can also include more specific information such as differences between aldehydes, ketones, and acids. `#FTIR` `#spectral-interpretation` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** Since each bond in an organic molecule potentially provides a distinct set of absorption peaks and there are a large number of such bonds in a typical organic molecule, spectra of compounds of interest tend to be rather complex and difficult to interpret. This requires comparison to known spectra within an organic IR spectra catalogue — akin to fingerprinting. `#FTIR` `#spectral-interpretation` `#fingerprinting` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** At times there may be a need to chemically separate components of a possible contamination source, such as a polymer having multiple components (cross-linked resins, dyes, release agents, plasticisers). Components that outgas can be collected by heating the sample, followed by IR analysis of components that come off at various temperatures. `#FTIR` `#polymer` `#outgassing` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** Infrared spectra are collected by passing IR light through a sample and measuring absorption as a function of wavelength. Liquids or emulsified solids are analysed by holding the material between crystals that do not absorb in the IR. Gases contained in a vessel with IR-transparent walls can also be analysed by measuring absorption of IR light passing through the vessel. `#FTIR` `#sample-preparation` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** In FTIR, the light is analysed with an interferometer; the resulting signal is the Fourier transform of the IR spectrum, converted back to the spectrum using a fast Fourier transform. Since all wavelengths of the spectrum are analysed at one time, it is significantly faster than dispersive methods. `#FTIR` `#interferometer` `#FFT` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** In microspot FTIR, a microscope creates a very small analysis area (as small as several microns), allowing analysis of small particles and very localised contamination. By integrating spectra over long times, spots of organic contamination smaller than the microscope field of view can be analysed — leaving only spatial resolution as a concern for some applications. This relatively limited spatial resolution compared to wafer fab particles has led to microspot FTIR applications being focused in assembly areas, where potential organic contaminants are widespread — making it an excellent match for a range of assembly-related issues. `#FTIR` `#microspot` `#assembly` `#spatial-resolution` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** Other techniques occasionally required in FA for specific niche analytical capability: XPS (X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy, also known as ESCA — Electron Spectroscopy for Chemical Analysis) observes electrons ejected from atoms due to interaction with monochromatic X-rays. Elemental characterisation is by binding energy (X-ray energy minus kinetic energy of the electron); binding energy is characteristic of elements. Molecular information is obtained from energy shifts with oxidation states — these shifts are much more pronounced than with Auger. The X-ray interaction is primarily with outer-shell electrons whose energies are most impacted by chemical surroundings. XPS is also a surface technique since, as with Auger, the probability of electron escape without energy loss from below the surface is low. `#XPS` `#ESCA` `#surface-analysis` `#oxidation-states` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** When non-localised ionic contamination is suspected, ion chromatography is used. Ions are typically extracted from the sample (a group of packages or wafer) with a common method such as boiling water extraction, concentrated, and then analysed on an ion chromatography column. Both anions and cations can be analysed on different types of columns. `#ion-chromatography` `#ionic-contamination` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** Residual Gas Analysis (RGA) provides a method for assessing the composition of gas in a cavity package. The package is ruptured in a vacuum (may require mechanical thinning to facilitate rupture) and gases escaping from the package are analysed with a quadrupole mass spectrometer. Most commonly used to detect moisture in the package, or air rather than the sealed gas (typically nitrogen) in the cavity — used to confirm a leak. `#RGA` `#cavity-package` `#moisture` `#hermeticity` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** Atomic Absorption Spectrometry (AAS) is commonly used to assess the purity of metals and is employed in bonding wire analysis, providing excellent sensitivity for low concentrations of metallic impurities in bonding wire. `#AAS` `#bonding-wire` `#purity` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** Three key characteristics of the electron beam for EDS are spot size, accelerating potential, and beam current. Spot size is not as critical a factor for EDS as for SEM — the sampled volume for EDS is most of the interaction volume of the electron beam, which is large relative to the electron spot size on the surface. Decreasing spot size does not have a comparable effect on the analysis area. `#EDS` `#spot-size` `#electron-beam` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** Variation of the accelerating potential has several impacts on X-ray generation: higher energy electrons provide more efficient X-ray generation and can excite higher energy X-rays (X-rays cannot be created with an energy higher than the incident electron energy). On the other hand, increasing the accelerating potential causes penetration of electrons into the sample to increase, enlarging the sampled volume — which degrades spatial resolution and usually increases the background signal. `#EDS` `#accelerating-voltage` `#X-ray-generation` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** Beam currents typically used for high-resolution SEM do not provide enough X-rays for efficient spectra acquisition; beam current must be increased to generate sufficient X-rays. Factors limiting the amount of beam current that can be effectively utilised include detector dead time, sample heating, and charging. `#EDS` `#beam-current` `#dead-time` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** Dead time of the detector is the percentage of time during which the detector is not measuring X-rays. Factors contributing to dead time include signal processing time in the preamplifier and rejection of multiple X-ray events — based on the notion that X-rays are detected as separate events (pulse height), systems must have software to reject multiple X-rays. Dead times on the order of 60% are desired. `#EDS` `#dead-time` `#detector` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** Detectors and detector windows: X-rays are detected by electron–hole generation in the Si(Li) or Ge detector. The bias across the detector results in a current flow proportional to the number of electron–hole pairs generated, which is proportional to the energy of the incident X-ray — approximately 3.8–3.9 eV of X-ray energy dissipated per electron–hole pair generated. `#EDS` `#SiLi-detector` `#electron-hole-pairs` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** Since competing events in the detector (such as heat generation) produce statistical variation in the number of electron–hole pairs generated for identical X-rays, this results in a normal distribution of peak heights. Peaks are sorted by pulse height as measured by the carrier collected during each event. `#EDS` `#detector` `#peak-distribution` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** A dead zone occurs at the top surface of the detector because lithium introduced is not able to totally neutralise surface defects in the silicon, resulting in traps near the surface. These combine with carriers to reduce the current between electrodes, producing a low-energy tail superimposed on the normal distribution of the peak. `#EDS` `#detector` `#dead-zone` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** Another feature of the Si(Li) detector is escape peaks: if a silicon Kα X-ray is generated by the incident X-ray and escapes from the detector, the total energy available for electron–hole generation is reduced by the 1.74 keV of the silicon X-ray. Escape occurs at the surface of the detector; escape peaks are normally on the order of a few percent of the real peak and can be corrected by subtraction from the spectra. `#EDS` `#escape-peaks` `#SiLi-detector` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.11]** The detector environment is critical: it must be maintained at liquid nitrogen temperatures to reduce shot noise and kept in a clean environment. The vacuum for the detector is typically maintained separately from the sample chamber and requires a window to protect the detector. The standard window is beryllium, which transmits higher energy X-rays but absorbs lower energy X-rays — limiting detection to elements with atomic number 11 (Na) and above. `#EDS` `#detector` `#Be-window` `#cryogenic` *(2026-06-25)*

- **[Ch.13]** Auger Electron Spectroscopy (AES) provides elemental identification and in some cases chemical information about substances in highly localised areas in very near-surface regions of materials, thin films, or film interfaces. It offers excellent spatial resolution as low as 200 Å diameter, surface analysis of the uppermost 20–50 Å of the sample surface, moderate sensitivity with detection limits in the 0.1–1.0% range, and depth profiling achieved by specimen sputtering. `#AES` `#surface-analysis` `#spatial-resolution` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.13]** In essence, a beam of primary electrons bombards a solid specimen surface, inducing energetic processes including removal of electrons from inner electronic orbitals. This creates an energy imbalance for each atom where an electron vacancy is induced. The imbalance is satisfied by electron rearrangement — an electron from the next higher energy level decays into the vacancy, resulting in either an X-ray or an energetic electron known as an Auger electron. `#AES` `#Auger-process` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.13]** Spot analysis is the most direct and often-used mode, providing a profile of specimen composition as a function of depth. Rastering the beam helps reduce charging and causes less local surface damage to the specimen. `#AES` `#spot-analysis` `#depth-profiling` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.13]** Spectra are typically displayed in one of four modes: N(E) vs E (energy distribution of detected electrons), dN(E)/dE vs E (differential mode), d[E×N(E)]/dE, or E×N(E) form. Since AES peaks are generally superimposed on a large energy background, they are most easily resolved in differential mode. `#AES` `#spectral-modes` `#differential` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.13]** It is often best to work with the least-processed data possible, e.g. N(E) or E×N(E) directly, which is advantageous for better signal-to-noise ratio and best possible energy resolution of the energy analyser. Since the measured signal is directly proportional to the number of atoms in the specimen, these forms provide the best information for quantitative analysis. `#AES` `#quantitative-analysis` `#SNR` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.13]** Example Auger energy calculation: energy difference in vacancy filling = 1844 eV − 104 eV (L₃ band) = 1740 eV. Energy loss in removing the Auger electron from the atom and then from the silicon surface = 104 eV + 17 eV (L₃ band) = 121 eV. Ejected electron energy = 1740 − 121 = 1619 eV, which identifies the electron as coming from a silicon atom. `#AES` `#energy-calculation` `#silicon` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.13]** Specimen, material, and AES operational concerns: a primary electron beam of 2 nA focused onto a 0.1 µm (1000 Å, 100 nm) diameter spot produces a current density of about 20 A/cm². Insulating materials cannot dissipate this much current — charge accumulates, often altering characteristic AES elemental peak positions and affecting the certainty of elemental identification. Surface heating can physically alter the composition of the surface by desorbing or even melting materials. As in all beam analysis methods, insulating dielectric films in IC devices are troublesome in AES due to their tendency to accumulate charge. `#AES` `#charging` `#current-density` `#dielectrics` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.13]** Different elements in compounds sputter under argon or other ion bombardment at different rates. An analysed surface can become depleted of elements with higher preferential sputtering rates, altering the specimen surface and giving false composition results. A known consequence of sputter depth profiling is physical roughening of the sputtered crater surface, which can equal or exceed the typical escape depth of Auger electrons and complicate interpretation of film surface composition or interfacial information. `#AES` `#preferential-sputtering` `#depth-profiling` `#artifacts` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.13]** Due to the surface analysis nature of AES, adsorbed gases and surface oxidation can significantly alter spectra even though they can be sputtered away — high vacuum is required to prevent reoccurrence. Specimen materials and their surfaces must be stable and not desorb or outgas in high vacuum; otherwise AES data will not truly represent the sample's surface condition. Samples with significant vapour pressure of their own are difficult to analyse by AES as they can degrade vacuum levels, damage vacuum pumps, or contaminate analysis systems. `#AES` `#vacuum` `#outgassing` `#surface-oxidation` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.13]** Relatively thin films of contamination on a bond pad can prevent reliable bonding. AES is well suited to identify such surface films and their thickness by depth profiling. Pads with incompletely removed passivation on their surfaces are a typical problem — spectra may show not only aluminium but also a silicon peak at 76 eV (indicative of SiO₂) and a peak at 120 eV due to phosphorus-doped oxide passivation used on the device. `#AES` `#bond-pad` `#contamination` `#passivation` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.13]** Another readily analysed problem is localised areas of aluminium oxyfluoride, formed during bond pad patterning by hydrofluoric acid etch solutions. Conversely, pads with discoloration (actually caused by scattering of incident light by a roughened surface texture) can be shown by AES to be quite clean aluminium surfaces with normal levels of native oxide. `#AES` `#bond-pad` `#oxyfluoride` `#surface-texture` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.13]** Bond lift failures are frequently analysed by AES — separated surfaces on both the bond pad and bonding surface of the lifted bond can provide useful information. `#AES` `#bond-lift` `#failure-analysis` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.13]** Cleanliness and surface composition of bond wire attachments to the header leadframe are critical for reliable bonding. AES has been used to identify the connection between nickel surface contamination and bondability to gold surfaces. Nickel can be co-plated with gold as an impurity from plating baths; nickel underplating can also diffuse through gold grain boundaries, covering gold surfaces and oxidising to make the surface non-bondable. `#AES` `#nickel-contamination` `#gold-plating` `#bondability` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.13]** Adhesion is also important for other parts of the assembly process including die attachment and solder adhesion. - **[Ch.14]** Secondary Ion Mass Spectrometry (SIMS): ability to detect all elements in the periodic table, excellent elemental sensitivity, and inherent depth profiling capabilities. Applications include dopant profiling, mobile ion monitoring, process contamination diagnosis, thin film characterisation, interface analysis, and surface analysis. `#SIMS` `#capabilities` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.14]** SIMS in preventive mode includes evaluation of the effectiveness of cleaning processes, monitoring the impact of new processing tools on wafer contamination levels, and implant matching studies for technology transfer between sites. `#SIMS` `#preventive` `#process-monitoring` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.14]** Basic theory and instrumentation: SIMS uses a focused primary ion beam (generally 1–20 keV) to erode atoms from a selected region of a sample surface. A charge exchange in the near-surface environment converts a portion of eroded atoms to negative or positive ions. Secondary ions are extracted via an electrical potential and subsequently analysed by a mass spectrometer. `#SIMS` `#ion-beam` `#mass-spectrometer` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.14]** SIMS yields elemental specificity with detection limits in the ppm–ppb range (atomic concentration). Quantification is accomplished by comparison of relative ion yields from a sample of interest to those of ion-implanted reference samples in the same matrix. Reproducibility approaching 1% can be achieved under ideal instrument conditions. `#SIMS` `#sensitivity` `#quantification` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.14]** The current density of the primary ion beam can be varied over several orders of magnitude to produce sample milling rates ranging from a few Å/hr to several thousand Å/min — making SIMS a powerful surface analysis, bulk analysis, thin film characterisation, and depth profiling tool. `#SIMS` `#milling-rate` `#depth-profiling` *(2026-06-25)*

## Sample Preparation & Techniques

- **[Ch.7]** A circuit repair/edit is performed by a series of cuts and conductive straps between probe points. Dielectric deposition facilitates the creation of these straps by allowing passivation of exposed metal (which is not contacted), and can cover deposited metal to reduce subsequent deposition lengths. If the area around the conductive strap is dark, it indicates that deposition overspray (typical of all FIB conductor depositions) has been cleaned up with halogen-assisted milling. *(2026-06-23)*
- **[Ch.7]** Cuts with an isolation resistance of up to 1 T ohms can be achieved using gas-assisted etching (GAE) with halogen gases, which addresses leakage caused by redeposition and gallium implantation. *(2026-06-23)*
- **[Ch.7]** Gate oxide damage from primary ion beam charging during FIB is mitigated in most cases because gallium implantation induces sufficient surface leakage current. For highly sensitive devices, charging can be overcome using a thin conductive carbon coating or a low-energy electron flood gun. *(2026-06-23)*
- **[Ch.7]** To avoid creating floating gates, FIB operations should be planned to follow a specific sequence: via contacts first, then deposition, and finally isolation cuts. This prevents leaving floating conductors mid-sequence. *(2026-06-23)*

- **[Ch.8]** Wet etching is the oldest method for IC deprocessing and is still frequently used. Control of etch rates is primarily accomplished by temperature, time, and concentration of the etchant solution. Good selectivity is desired — the etchant is selected based on its ability to etch the target material while minimizing damage to the underlying layer and other critical features. For failure analysis it is considered a global technique; its primary issues are selectivity and its isotropic nature which causes unwanted undercutting, narrow metal lines tending to lift off the surface, and etching of materials through holes such as vias (known as "etch-through"). Etch-through has been greatly reduced with the advent of via plug technology. Additionally, the selectivity of metal etches to most dielectrics is very good, and etch-through instances are frequently halted by barrier/adhesion layers and tungsten plugs in vias, making it less of an issue during deprocessing. *(2026-06-24)*
- **[Ch.8]** Silicon etching and crystal defect decoration are still commonly performed with wet etches. Applications summarized below: *(2026-06-24)*

  | Common Materials  | Common Etchants                       | Selectivity                    | Comments                                              |
  |-------------------|----------------------------------------|--------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------|
  | Silicon           | HF with oxidizers (HNO₃ / chromic acid)| —                              | Most commonly used; very caustic; chrome is heavy metal|
  | Silicon oxides    | HF buffered with BH₄F                 | Moderate to metals             |                                                       |
  | Silicon oxides    | 10:1 HF                               | Moderate                       | Attacks silicon if left too long                      |
  | Silicon nitride   | Nitretch                               | High to metals, low for CVD oxides | 45–50 °C, sensitive to temp                       |
  | Silicon nitride   | HPO₃                                   | Poor                           | Caustic, etches Al                                    |
  | Silicon nitride   | Buffered HF                            | Poor                           | Etches oxides                                         |

  | Aluminium         | HPO₃ / HNO₃ / acetic acid             | Good                           | Must be heated ~70 °C                                 |

- **[Ch.8]** Major drawback of a barrel etcher is the lack of temperature control, causing both etch rate and fluorocarbon polymer formation to increase as the sample gets hotter over time. Since it is primarily isotropic, the effects of undercutting have not been alleviated. *(2026-06-24)*
- **[Ch.8]** The subsequent parallel plate etcher improves upon the barrel etcher by providing temperature control for added stability. The sample is placed on the grounded electrode, with parallel plate electrodes at the top and bottom inside the vacuum chamber. This configuration takes advantage of ions created in the plasma by creating a steady-state electric field supplied by a low-frequency 450 kHz RF signal to the top electrode through a capacitor (to block DC current). The small DC bias between the plates from the RF source is insufficient to initiate ion movement on its own; the RF signal is also important in initiating movement of the negatively charged anode at the chamber bottom due to the high plasma pressure. This directional ion bombardment means polymer formation is now restricted to features parallel only to the direction of ion movement (i.e., sidewalls), thus providing anisotropic etching characteristics. In the parallel configuration, CF₃ radicals produced near the electrodes — which were too far away in a barrel etcher — are now close enough to etch oxide at a fairly high rate. However, bombardment also occurs on the top electrode; sputtered metal from the top electrode can deposit on the samples under etch, inhibiting etching as it accumulates. *(2026-06-24)*
- **[Ch.8]** The reactive ion etcher (RIE) has three subtle differences from the parallel plate etcher: (1) The chamber operates at much lower pressures achieved by a turbomolecular pump (~10 mPa vs 100–500 mPa for parallel etching), allowing reaction by-products to be pumped away much more quickly and less likely to deposit on the sample. (2) A much higher frequency 13.56 MHz RF source supplies the energy to create the plasma, providing a larger electric field between the two plates. (3) The grounded anode is designed with a surface area ~2× larger than the cathode, and their positions are swapped so the sample now sits on the cathode at the bottom of the chamber. This asymmetry in electrode area creates a larger potential drop across the capacitance between the lower cathode and the plasma than the upper anode to the plasma, resulting in more ion bombardment to the sample on the lower plate and less sputtering of the metal anode at the top. *(2026-06-24)*
- **[Ch.8]** These RIE improvements provide enhanced control over selectivity and anisotropy. When metal etching, compounds of chlorine (instead of fluorine) are used — the gases are NOT interchangeable as with dielectrics. Chlorine-based RIE provides greater control for removing aluminium; upper-layer aluminium metal is removed uniformly with no impact to the underlying metal layer, as would typically be observed during wet etching. *(2026-06-24)*
- **[Ch.8]** Silver lining: with the incorporation of tungsten plug technology to connect different levels of metal, such metal RIE using highly toxic chlorine is no longer a necessity. Wet etching is much more convenient and accessible to use and does not affect the tungsten plug. *(2026-06-24)*
- **[Ch.8]** A peculiar observation during RIE is "RIE grass" — an etching artifact caused by redeposition of sputtered materials in small patches onto the sample. These patches are inert to the chemically active species in the plasma and effectively behave as micromasks, preventing the underlying film from etching evenly. It can occur in several ways: redeposition of sputtered IC metallization exposed during ion bombardment (aluminium grass, in the presence of oxygen and fluorine); polymer grass as a normal by-product of standard plasma etch chemistry (minimized by adding oxygen or reducing gas pressure); and redeposition of IC package material such as gold that is not well protected during RIE and redeposits on the sample surface. *(2026-06-24)*
- **[Ch.8]** The third method for deprocessing is mechanical polishing (also known as parallel polishing or lapping) — the physical deprocessing of an IC through the use of abrasive materials, which is time-consuming and meticulous by nature. With increasing layers of thin films, it has become more competitive with chemical deprocessing in terms of overall deprocessing time, as the number of required deprocessing steps increases and deprocessing-induced artifacts accumulate, making mechanical polishing more attractive. Increasingly smaller feature sizes amplify the probability of unwanted metal lifting during dry and wet chemical deprocessing. However, the major drawback of lapping is the difficulty in maintaining planarity of the surface, especially over a larger region. If the defective area of the die has been limited by failure site isolation techniques, mechanical polishing is an effective alternative to chemical deprocessing. *(2026-06-24)*
- **[Ch.8]** From an inspection perspective, the advantage of polishing over wet and dry etching is the ability to stop mid-layer and view features in the area of interest within the same plane. With both wet and dry etching, it is not possible to view metal and oxide at the same plane because removal of oxide would leave only the topography of the metal. Mechanical polishing is especially useful on multilayer interconnect processes with advanced planarization techniques. With a very planarized process, it is often difficult to discern critical features when viewed from the top due to lack of depth perception. Polishing allows visual inspection of protruding processing defects such as short-causing stringers, filaments, and particles that might otherwise be lost during a wet or dry etch procedure. *(2026-06-24)*
- **[Ch.9]** Cross-sectioning provides information about certain types of defects that are inaccessible by deprocessing: (1) failures occurring at the bottom of etched holes such as vias and contacts are not readily observed after deprocessing; (2) issues involving etch contours or the thickness of thin films are not usually resolved by deprocessing; and (3) for many assembly-related defects internal to the package, cross-sectioning is the only way to directly observe the problem. *(2026-06-25)*

- **[Ch.9]** Polishing suspensions are available as liquids and sprays, which can also be prepared from powders. The abrasive materials are typically aluminium oxide and diamond. *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.9]** Glass wheels are a plate of hard lime glass with surface irregularities limited to much less than 1 µm. They produce a fine polished surface on typical semiconductor samples, can last many months, and require only periodic light detergent scrubbings to remove accumulated material. - **[Ch.9]** Semiconductor wafers are processed with single-crystal silicon, usually having a ⟨100⟩ or ⟨111⟩ lattice plane surface. CMOS most commonly uses ⟨100⟩, resulting in cleave planes along the wafer's x and y axes. *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.9]** Once the desired area is located, the desired cleave line is followed to the edge of the wafer and a scribe mark is made using a diamond scribe in the same direction as the cleave line. A scribe mark about 5 mm in length with 1 or 2 firm strokes deep is sufficient. *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.9]** A consistent method for breaking the cleave is to place the end of a straightened metal paper clip under the scribe line along the direction of the desired cleave, followed by slight downward pressure at the edges of the wafer a few centimetres away from the scribe line. This can obtain an accuracy of a few millimetres. *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.9]** Polishing can be ineffective for defects or layers that are at or very close to the surface — these surface features often break off during polishing. For this reason, a layer of protective oxide or nitride is often deposited over the surface to provide more mechanical stability to the area. This can be performed with spin-on glass or a plasma deposition system for a larger area, or even a FIB system for a point defect. *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.9]** Staining has become a general term applied to chemical delineation or decoration of device features, particularly in cross-section, where its main purpose is to provide contrast when imaging various device features. Cross-sections do exhibit some natural contrast — colour in optical microscopy, and atomic number and charging contrasts in SEM. *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.9]** Many etchants used in deprocessing are also useful for cross-section staining of the same materials. Oxide etches typically involve HF (hydrofluoric acid) buffered with NH₄F (ammonium fluoride). Etch rates depend on the density and doping of the oxide. *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.9]** Common Oxide Etch (COE) is a mixture of 6% HF (49 wt%), 35% NH₄F (40 wt%), and 59% water by volume. Another widely used oxide etch is Standard Oxide Etch (SOE), which consists of 50% glacial acetic acid, 5% ammonium fluoride (40 wt%), and 45% water by volume. Typical etch times are ~20 seconds for a light delineation effect or up to 1 minute for a heavier delineation. *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.9]** Within FIB, dual-column systems make it possible to mill with the FIB while simultaneously observing the sidewall of the cross-section with a FESEM. CAD navigation facilitates the use of device layouts to quickly locate an area to be sectioned; wafer mapping capability allows rapid localisation of defects identified by various in-line wafer fab tools. Improvements in ion beam resolution enable sectioning of sub-micron features with precision and repeatability not achievable by mechanical sectioning techniques. The availability of gas injection for deposition and enhanced etching in FIB has increased the flexibility and efficiency of FIB cross-sectioning. *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.9]** The primary limitation with FIB sectioning is that milling time scales linearly with the length of the section; for section lengths of 50 µm or more, other techniques tend to be more practical. A second issue is beam damage to the sample — redeposition and gallium implantation alter the surface being inspected, and charging from the ion beam has been observed to cause electrical overstress damage to some isolated structures. *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.9]** Related FIB uses: surface defects or soft materials (like aluminium or photoresist) can break off or smear during conventional polishing — FIB can cut cleanly through them and progressively section through a feature, efficiently providing a succession of views. Both the ion beam and electron beam in a dual-column system can be conveniently used for final imaging, each providing unique information due to different contrast mechanisms. Alternatively, FIB may perform limited deprocessing such as opening up an area so that elemental analysis can be obtained. *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.9]** FIB sectioning can be combined with cleaving or polishing — useful when the sectioning precision of FIB is required but the final imaging needs a true perpendicular view or resolution better than achievable in the FIB. Since a FIB section is a rectangular hole, the wall of the cross-section must be viewed at an angle off orthogonal. In practice, the sample is cleaved or polished to within a few microns of the point of interest, then FIB mills out the remaining material to the desired location. Based on this concept, FIB can also aid in TEM sample preparation. *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.9]** TEM tends to be the technique of choice for very high resolution imaging of cross-sections, used to identify many defect types that are not observable with a FESEM on standard cross-sections. *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.9]** Thin layers of contamination (such as in vias or contacts), gate oxide non-uniformity, and subtle etching problems are typical issues requiring TEM. It is also a useful tool for identification of silicon stacking faults and dislocations, and chemical phases — since lattice spacings of crystalline materials can be resolved. Such lattice spacings are used for magnification calibration, which permits very accurate measurements of layer thickness. *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.9]** The main drawback of TEM is the size limitation of the sample: the sample must fit on a grid that is 3 mm in diameter, and the area of interest must be thinned to be transparent to electrons. The thickness requirement is often stated to be less than 100 nm, but for best results the sample thickness should be closer to 50 nm. The majority of effort for TEM viewing is in preparing the sample to meet these dimension requirements. *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.9]** A common technique for preparing TEM cross-section samples is known as "multiple stacking." It is useful in instances where a precise section line is not required — such as large arrays or general layer viewing — thereby allowing preparation of multiple sites at one time. *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.9]** Samples are typically cut by a diamond saw into pieces 3 mm × 1.5 mm. If there are fewer than 4 samples with areas of interest, additional dummy pieces are used to bring the total up to 4; each piece can be used to inspect a different area. They are glued into a stack using an appropriate adhesive (low viscosity and minimal outgassing, such as a phenolic epoxy) with the total thickness of all pieces limited to 2 mm. *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.9]** Exceptions: more than 4 pieces can be included in a stack as long as the total thickness fits within the viewable area of the TEM grid, although this has typically limited the number to 6. Backside grinding of the pieces has allowed many more samples to be processed in the same stack. A key distinction is that the "fronts" of the pieces are oriented in a non-symmetric pattern to permit unambiguous identification of each piece when imaged by TEM. *(2026-06-25)*

## General / Miscellaneous

- **[Ch.]** IC manufacturing refresher — well formation: The p-type substrate is typically lightly doped to provide a background concentration that gives good n-channel transistor characteristics, but not so high as to require an overly high n-dopant concentration to overcome the p-dopant when creating the n-well. However, the low voltage threshold and high depletion width spreading created by a lightly doped p-type substrate allows for parasitic NMOS channel formation and potential punch-through problems. To mitigate possible channel formation, additional boron implants or "twin-well" processes (which support formation of both transistor types) are frequently employed. Typically the pattern for well formation is defined in a thick thermally grown oxide of ~1000 Å. This oxide may eventually contribute to isolation between devices (preventing unwanted parasitic electrical paths), but often is removed prior to field implant followed by actual field oxide isolation formation. *(2026-06-24)*

- **[Ch.15]** Global failure site isolation — for memory elements (SRAM, DRAM, cache), global fail site isolation is usually accomplished with a bit fail map created from test data. Determining the logical and physical location responsible for failure is generally independent of changes in IC process technology. The limited density of interconnect wiring allows frontside execution of physical fail site isolation techniques like liquid crystal, photon emission microscopy, or charge-induced voltage alteration (CIVA). `#fail-site-isolation` `#memory` `#CIVA` `#future` *(2026-06-25)*

- **[Ch.15]** Thermal analysis with liquid crystal or fluorescent microthermal imaging (FMI) is limited from the backside by silicon's rapid diffusion of heat from pinpoint heat sources, some of which may be buried within interconnect layers. Infrared thermography, despite being diffraction-limited in spatial resolution to several microns, will offer some relief for isolation of relatively high heat sources. `#thermal-analysis` `#FMI` `#backside` `#future` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.15]** Precise thermal isolation lacks a tractable backside solution. One promising method takes advantage of local changes in silicon's optical properties with changes in temperature. This development is critical as certain defects generate only excess heat and may be undetectable with light emissions or other methods. `#thermal-isolation` `#backside` `#future` *(2026-06-25)*
- **[Ch.15]** Despite the apparent longevity of emission and hot spot techniques, two potential roadblocks lie ahead. At the same time, significantly smaller defects are anticipated to have higher resistance, reducing defect-related leakage.
